# National HR Guidance on PIP Handling and Disclosure Obligations
**Source:** Adapted from SHRM PIP Guidance Framework 2025 | **Classification:** Internal HR

## Overview

This document provides national HR guidance on the administration of performance
improvement plans (PIPs), including employee notification requirements, manager
training obligations, and disclosure requirements in the context of subsequent
employment applications and background diligence.

This document is background reference material for the diligence team. For the specific
policy section requiring PIP self-disclosure by candidates, see hr_policy/bg_check_policy_v4.md
Section 5.1.


## Guidance Section 1: PIP Administration — Topic 1

Under the doctrine of negligent hiring, an employer may be held liable for damages arising from an employee's wrongful acts if the employer knew, or should have known, of facts that would have made the hiring decision unreasonably risky. Thorough background diligence — including reference interviews, employment date verification, and professional profile review — is a recognized mitigation against negligent hiring claims.

The statute of limitations for negligent hiring claims varies by jurisdiction. In most US jurisdictions, the limitations period runs from the date of the harmful act by the employee, not the date of hire. Employers who can demonstrate a documented, multi-channel diligence process — including reference interviews that covered departure circumstances — are generally better positioned to defend against negligent hiring claims, even where a finding was made but the hire proceeded at a lower risk tier.

Performance improvement plans (PIPs) are internal management tools used by employers to document performance concerns and establish measurable improvement objectives for employees who are not meeting expectations. The existence of a PIP is generally treated as a confidential internal employment matter. However, where a candidate is asked to self-disclose recent PIPs as part of a background diligence process, failure to disclose a material PIP constitutes a misrepresentation under most employment law frameworks.

Section 6 — Risk Tier Framework. Low: no discrepancies, positive references, no PIP history. Medium: minor discrepancies within 30-day threshold, or guarded references with no adverse content. High: discrepancy exceeding 30-day threshold, OR undisclosed PIP/disciplinary action discovered, OR two concurrent moderate discrepancies. Hold: High-tier findings with deliberate misrepresentation, Major discrepancy (>90 days), or active legal matters.

Section 3.2 — Employment Date Discrepancy Thresholds (v4). Discrepancies of 30 days or fewer are classified as Minor and logged without escalation. Discrepancies exceeding 30 days but not exceeding 90 days are classified as Moderate and require escalation to the hiring manager with a written notation. Discrepancies exceeding 90 days are classified as Major and require escalation to senior HR leadership and legal review before any offer is extended.

## Guidance Section 4: PIP Administration — Topic 4

Employment verification law in the United States is primarily governed by the Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA), 15 U.S.C. § 1681 et seq., which imposes disclosure and authorization requirements on employers who use consumer reporting agencies (CRAs) to conduct background checks. Where an employer conducts background diligence using only in-house HR personnel and direct reference interviews — without engaging a CRA — the FCRA's procedural requirements do not apply. However, employers remain subject to applicable state employment verification laws, anti-discrimination statutes, and the implied covenant of fair dealing.
