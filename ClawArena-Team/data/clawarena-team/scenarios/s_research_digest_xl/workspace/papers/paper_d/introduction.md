# 1. Introduction

The dominant paradigm for aligning large language models with human preferences is reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). RLHF treats alignment as a preference-modelling problem: human annotators rank pairs of model outputs, a reward model is fitted to those rankings, and the language model is fine-tuned to maximise the reward. RLHF has been used to align all of the major deployed assistant models, and its successes are well-documented. Its costs, however, are substantial: each new alignment criterion typically requires a fresh round of human annotation, which is expensive, slow, and difficult to scale across the long tail of safety, factuality, and tone requirements that production systems must satisfy.

Constitutional AI is a complementary alignment paradigm that reduces the dependence on per-example human labels. Instead of asking annotators to rank outputs, Constitutional AI asks the *model itself* to critique and revise its own outputs according to a written set of principles — the "constitution". The constitution encodes the alignment goals in natural language ("the assistant should not produce toxic content", "the assistant should be helpful and honest", and so on), and the model uses these principles to filter or revise its responses during a multi-step self-critique loop. Constitutional AI has been shown to deliver alignment quality comparable to RLHF while requiring an order of magnitude less human labelling.

The Constitutional AI approach as originally proposed has two practical limitations that have hindered widespread adoption. First, the self-critique loop is expensive at training time, because each output requires multiple additional forward passes (one to generate, one or more to critique, one to revise). On a 13B-parameter model with a critique chain of length three, the training-time compute is roughly 4× that of standard RLHF. Second, the constitutional principles must be engineered by hand, and small differences in phrasing produce large differences in alignment behaviour. Practitioners have reported that finding a constitution that produces robust alignment requires substantial iteration, and the resulting principles are often fragile — they work well for the distributions they were tuned on and degrade silently on others.

In this paper we propose **DistillAlign**, which combines the best features of RLHF and Constitutional AI while avoiding the costs of each. The central idea is to *distill* the constitutional critique capability into a lightweight reward model, which then provides alignment guidance during a standard RLHF training loop. The reward model is trained once, on a synthetic preference dataset generated by running the full Constitutional AI critique loop on a smaller "tutor" model. Once trained, the reward model can be used to score generations from the larger student model with the same single-forward-pass cost as any other reward model, eliminating the per-generation critique overhead. The constitutional principles enter the system through the tutor's critique behaviour and become implicit in the reward model's parameters; they no longer have to be re-engineered for each new student.

DistillAlign retains the labelling efficiency of Constitutional AI (the constitution is the primary source of supervision; no per-example human labels are required), enjoys the training-time efficiency of RLHF (the reward model is queried once per generation), and exposes the same operational interface as standard RLHF (a fixed reward model, a standard PPO loop). It is designed to be a drop-in upgrade for organisations that already operate an RLHF pipeline.

Our contributions are: a method for distilling Constitutional AI critique into a small reward model; a careful evaluation showing large toxicity reductions on standard benchmarks and on production-derived adversarial prompts; an ablation study isolating the contributions of the critique chain depth, the tutor size, and the reward-model size; and an open-source release of both the trained reward model and the synthetic preference dataset used to train it.
