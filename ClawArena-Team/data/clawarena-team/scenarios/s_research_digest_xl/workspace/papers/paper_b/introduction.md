# 1. Introduction

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures have re-emerged as one of the most effective ways to scale the total parameter count of a language model without paying a proportional inference cost. By replacing each dense feed-forward block with a bank of `E` parallel "experts" and a gating function that activates only `k` of them per token (typically `k = 2`), an MoE model can hold an order of magnitude more parameters than a dense baseline while computing only a small fraction of them on any given forward pass. State-of-the-art MoE models such as Mixtral, GLaM, and Switch-Transformer have demonstrated quality competitive with much larger dense models at comparable training compute, and the trend toward MoE has accelerated as model sizes push the limits of single-device memory.

In production deployment, however, the practical throughput of MoE inference is frequently limited not by raw FLOPs but by two operational pathologies that the MoE literature has only partially addressed. The first is **load imbalance**: under realistic input distributions, a small subset of experts receives a disproportionate share of the routed tokens, leaving the other experts idle and the corresponding accelerators underutilised. Auxiliary load-balancing losses during training mitigate but do not eliminate this effect, especially at inference time when the input distribution shifts away from training. The second is **communication overhead**: when experts are sharded across multiple devices (the dominant deployment pattern for models that exceed single-device memory), every token must be sent to the device hosting its assigned expert and the resulting hidden state must be sent back. On networks slower than NVLink — including the PCIe and Ethernet links common in budget inference clusters — this all-to-all communication can dominate the wall-clock latency of an MoE forward pass.

The standard response to these pathologies is **expert parallelism**: each device owns a disjoint subset of experts, and the input batch is shuffled to route tokens to the appropriate device. While conceptually simple, expert parallelism suffers from both pathologies simultaneously. Imbalanced expert activation creates "straggler" devices that bottleneck the entire forward pass, and the all-to-all communication phase serialises the layer until every device has received every token it needs. Recent variants such as **tutel** and **DeepSpeed-MoE** introduce expert replication and capacity-factor tricks to dampen these effects, but they trade memory or latency for the imbalance mitigation, and on long-tailed input distributions the trade-off is unattractive.

In this paper, we propose a different deployment strategy that addresses both pathologies at their structural root rather than treating them after the fact. Our framework, **ClusterMoE**, rests on three observations. First, experts in a trained MoE model exhibit strong activation-pattern correlations: certain pairs of experts are almost always co-activated on the same input, while others are virtually never co-activated. Second, when correlated experts are placed on the same device, the all-to-all communication phase shrinks dramatically because most tokens stay local. Third, the residual communication that does cross device boundaries can be hidden under the per-device compute by an appropriately scheduled pipeline, eliminating the serialisation penalty of expert parallelism. ClusterMoE turns these observations into a concrete deployment procedure: it clusters experts by their empirical co-activation similarity, assigns each cluster to a device, and runs the resulting topology through a custom pipeline-parallel scheduler.

The contributions of this paper are as follows. We present the empirical study that motivates ClusterMoE, including a measurement of expert co-activation across Mixtral, Grok-1, and our own 70B-parameter pretrained MoE. We describe the clustering algorithm and the pipeline scheduler in sufficient detail for reproduction. We report comprehensive throughput and latency results on three model sizes, three hardware configurations, and six downstream benchmarks. Finally, we discuss the operational lessons we learned during the six-month deployment of ClusterMoE in a production inference service, including how to handle hot-swapping of experts and how to recover from network partitions in a way that preserves request-level consistency.

The paper is organised as follows. Section 2 reviews related work in MoE training and deployment. Section 3 describes the ClusterMoE clustering procedure and pipeline scheduler. Section 4 reports throughput, latency, and quality results, including a detailed analysis of the latency reduction. Section 5 covers limitations and deployment considerations. Section 6 concludes.
