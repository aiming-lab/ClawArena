# 1. Introduction

Large language models trained at scale acquire a broad parametric memory of facts, but they are not reliable repositories of factual information. Even the strongest models hallucinate with non-trivial frequency, asserting plausible-sounding statements that are simply false, and the errors are often presented with the same confident tone as correct answers. The cost of these errors depends on the application but can be substantial: hallucinated case citations, fabricated medical advice, and invented historical claims have all been documented in production deployments, and the difficulty of detecting hallucinations is a leading reason that organisations remain reluctant to deploy generative models in high-stakes domains.

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is the dominant industrial response to this problem. In a RAG pipeline, the model is supplied at inference time with passages retrieved from a curated corpus (Wikipedia, internal documentation, a vetted knowledge base), and is prompted to base its answer on the supplied passages. RAG works because the retrieved passages typically contain the relevant fact, and a sufficiently capable model can usually attend to and reproduce that fact in its answer. The approach has been deployed at scale in commercial assistants, search products, and enterprise document-question-answering systems.

Despite its widespread use, RAG has a well-documented failure mode that the literature refers to as **context drift**: even when the retrieved passages contain the correct answer, the model sometimes ignores them and falls back on its parametric knowledge, which may be outdated, incomplete, or simply wrong. We have measured context drift on a panel of strong open-weight and closed-weight models and find that the rate of drift on the TruthfulQA benchmark exceeds 22% even when relevant passages are present in the prompt. The result is a system that *appears* grounded — passages are retrieved and shown to the user — but is not actually faithful to them.

The root cause of context drift is the standard decoding objective. Maximum-likelihood decoding selects the token that the model assigns highest probability conditioned on the prompt and the retrieved context. This probability is a smooth combination of the model's parametric beliefs and the in-context evidence, with weights that the model itself implicitly chooses. When the parametric belief is wrong but confidently held, the maximum-likelihood token can disagree with the retrieved passage. No amount of better retrieval can address this — the retrieved passages may be perfect, and the model may still ignore them.

In this paper, we propose **FaithRAG**, a framework that addresses context drift by changing the decoding objective rather than the retrieval. FaithRAG introduces a *constrained decoding* term that penalises tokens that are not *attributable* to the retrieved passages, where attribution is operationalised by a lightweight scoring model that we train alongside the language model. The constraint is implemented at decoding time and does not require modifying the generator's weights, which makes it easy to compose with any existing RAG pipeline. The penalty is differentiable and we expose a temperature parameter that allows applications to trade off fluency against strict faithfulness as required.

The main contributions of this paper are: (1) a formal definition of context drift and a measurement protocol that allows it to be tracked separately from retrieval-induced errors; (2) the FaithRAG decoding objective, including the attribution scorer and the constraint integration; (3) a comprehensive empirical evaluation on TruthfulQA, FActScore, and our own held-out enterprise QA benchmark, demonstrating large factual-accuracy gains; and (4) a study of the fluency-faithfulness trade-off, showing that strict faithfulness is achievable with only modest fluency cost.
