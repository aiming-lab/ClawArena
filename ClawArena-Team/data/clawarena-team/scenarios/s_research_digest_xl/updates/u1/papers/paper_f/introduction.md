# 1. Introduction

Compositional generalisation is the ability to combine known primitives in novel ways. A speaker who knows the verb "to jump" and the modifier "twice" should understand "jump twice" even if they have never heard that exact phrase before. Compositional generalisation is foundational to human language use and to systematic reasoning more broadly, and it has been an explicit goal of natural-language-understanding research since at least the introduction of formal semantics in the 1970s.

Despite the enormous practical success of large language models on standard benchmarks, their compositional generalisation behaviour remains uneven. On benchmarks specifically designed to test compositional generalisation — SCAN, COGS, CFQ — even very strong neural models fall well short of the 100% accuracy that their humans-author counterparts can achieve, often dropping by 30 to 50 percentage points relative to in-distribution evaluation. The pattern of errors is revealing: neural models can handle novel primitives in familiar combinations and familiar primitives in slightly novel combinations, but they break down on genuinely novel compositions that require systematically applying rules they have learned to inputs they have not seen.

Neurosymbolic approaches have been advanced as the principled fix. By coupling a neural encoder, which handles the messy and ambiguous mapping from surface forms to meanings, with an explicit symbolic reasoner, which guarantees systematic composition over a clean abstract syntax, a neurosymbolic system can in principle inherit the strengths of both paradigms. In practice, however, neurosymbolic systems have struggled with two issues. The first is **engineering effort**: many prior systems require hand-crafted grammars, domain-specific symbolic vocabularies, or tailored pipelines that resist transfer between domains. The second is **error propagation**: an upstream parsing mistake at the neural side produces an incorrect symbolic form, which the downstream reasoner then dutifully executes, producing a confidently wrong answer that is difficult to recover from.

Recent advances in pretrained language models suggest that the engineering-effort problem can now be solved differently. Rather than training a domain-specific parser, we can prompt a strong general-purpose language model to produce a target representation in a standard formalism (we use the typed lambda calculus, but the approach generalises to other formalisms). The neural parser is then itself a frozen pretrained model that does not require any domain-specific training data; only the prompt and the symbolic vocabulary need to be specified.

The error-propagation problem we address by a combination of architectural and training-time techniques. Specifically, we train the parser to produce *multiple* candidate parses for each input, executable in parallel by the symbolic reasoner; the reasoner's execution traces are then ranked using a learned verifier model. The verifier is small enough to be trained on synthetic data and serves as the bridge between the neural and symbolic components, dampening the cost of any individual parsing error.

We instantiate these ideas in **NSR** (Neurosymbolic Reasoner), a system that combines a pretrained language model parser, a typed lambda calculus executor, and a verifier model. NSR is evaluated on three compositional generalisation benchmarks — SCAN, COGS, and CFQ — and achieves 91% average accuracy, an 18-percentage-point improvement over the best neural-only baseline. NSR also generalises to unseen operator *combinations* without additional training, recovering the systematic composition property that neural models fundamentally lack.

The contributions of this paper are: (1) the NSR architecture, including the multiple-parse decoding strategy and the verifier model; (2) a detailed evaluation on three compositional generalisation benchmarks; (3) an analysis of error patterns that distinguishes NSR's failure modes from those of neural baselines; and (4) an open-source release of the system, the verifier, and the synthetic data used to train it.

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section 2 discusses related work in neurosymbolic systems and compositional generalisation. Section 3 describes the NSR architecture. Section 4 reports the experimental results. Section 5 discusses limitations, and Section 6 concludes.
