# 1. Introduction

The personalisation potential of large language models is increasingly limited by privacy considerations. Tasks such as adapting an assistant to the language of a specific medical specialty, fine-tuning a customer-support model on a company's internal correspondence, or training a writing assistant on a user's own drafts all benefit from access to data that is, by its nature, sensitive. The straightforward approach — collecting the data centrally and fine-tuning a model on it — is increasingly incompatible with privacy regulation (GDPR, HIPAA, and similar frameworks), enterprise data-governance policies, and end-user expectations. Even when collection is legally permissible, recent attacks have shown that fine-tuned language models can leak training data through their output distributions, exposing concrete examples to anyone who queries the model.

Federated learning offers a credible alternative. In a federated setup, the training data remains on the device or institution that owns it, and only model updates (gradients or weight deltas) are communicated to a central aggregator. The aggregator combines the updates and produces a new global model, which is then shipped back to the clients for the next round of local training. Federated learning has been deployed at scale for keyboard prediction and a small number of other low-stakes models, but it has not been widely adopted for large-language-model fine-tuning because of two persistent technical challenges.

The first challenge is **client data heterogeneity**. Federated clients almost never have identically distributed data — one client's emails differ in vocabulary, length, and style from another's. Standard federated optimisers such as FedAvg average client updates, and when those updates point in different directions due to heterogeneity, the average degrades. The phenomenon is known as "client drift", and on heterogeneous distributions it can cost more than 20 percentage points of accuracy relative to a centralised baseline. Recent variants such as FedProx and SCAFFOLD reduce but do not eliminate the drift, especially in the high-heterogeneity regime that characterises real-world deployments.

The second challenge is **formal privacy**. Federated learning by itself does not provide differential privacy: a curious aggregator can in principle infer information about individual training examples from the gradients it receives. Adding differential privacy (DP) requires injecting calibrated noise into the gradients, which further degrades accuracy. Reconciling DP with federated learning on heterogeneous data is the central technical difficulty, and most existing systems sacrifice one of the two goals rather than achieving both.

In this paper we propose **FedAlign**, a federated fine-tuning framework that addresses both challenges together. FedAlign combines DP-SGD-style gradient clipping and noise injection with a novel **alignment regulariser** that encourages each client's local update to be consistent with a learned summary of the global model's behaviour, without requiring any data sharing. The regulariser is parameter-free — it operates entirely on the model's own intermediate representations and a small set of public anchor inputs — and it materially reduces client drift in the heterogeneous-data regime where vanilla FedAvg performs worst.

The empirical result of the paper is a substantial closing of the gap between federated and centralised fine-tuning. Under a strict differential privacy guarantee of (ε=2, δ=10⁻⁵), FedAlign achieves an accuracy drop of only 8% relative to centralised, non-private fine-tuning, compared with 27% for vanilla DP-FedAvg under the same privacy budget. This is, to our knowledge, the smallest reported gap for differentially-private federated fine-tuning of large language models.

The contributions of this paper are: (1) a formal description of the alignment regulariser, including its compatibility with DP-SGD noise injection; (2) a comprehensive evaluation on three federated benchmarks and three data-distribution settings; (3) a theoretical analysis of the regulariser's effect on convergence under heterogeneous data; and (4) an open-source implementation that integrates with two popular federated learning frameworks. The rest of the paper is organised as follows. Section 2 reviews related work; Section 3 describes FedAlign; Section 4 reports results; Section 5 discusses limitations; Section 6 concludes.
