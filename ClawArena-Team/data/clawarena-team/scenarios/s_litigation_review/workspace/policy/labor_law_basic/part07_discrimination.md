# Part VII — Anti-Discrimination Framework

## 7.1 Protected Characteristics

The principal anti-discrimination statutes prohibit employment decisions made
on the basis of, among other characteristics: race, color, ethnicity, national
origin, religion, sex, gender identity, gender expression, sexual orientation,
age (above a statutory threshold), disability, pregnancy, marital status,
family status, veteran status, and genetic information. The list of protected
characteristics varies by jurisdiction.

## 7.2 Disparate Treatment

Disparate treatment occurs where the employer treats an employee less
favorably because of a protected characteristic. Disparate treatment may be
proved by direct evidence — such as a discriminatory statement by a
decision-maker — or, more commonly, by circumstantial evidence under the
established burden-shifting framework.

A pretextual reason for an adverse employment action, when coupled with
evidence of a protected characteristic, may support an inference of
discriminatory motive.

## 7.3 Disparate Impact

Disparate impact arises where a facially neutral employment practice has a
disproportionate adverse effect on a protected group and is not justified by
business necessity. The disparate impact analysis is a statistical one and
typically requires expert evidence.

## 7.4 Harassment

Harassment on the basis of a protected characteristic is a form of
prohibited discrimination. Harassment is actionable where it is severe or
pervasive enough to alter the terms and conditions of employment and create
a hostile work environment. Quid pro quo harassment — that is, conditioning
employment benefits on the acceptance of unwelcome conduct — is actionable
on a single instance.

## 7.5 Retaliation

Retaliation against an employee for opposing a discriminatory practice,
filing a charge, or participating in an investigation or proceeding is
prohibited. A claim of retaliation may succeed even where the underlying
discrimination claim does not.

## 7.6 Mixed Motive

Where an adverse employment action is shown to have been motivated in part
by a protected characteristic, liability may attach even though other,
lawful, motives also played a role. The remedies available in a mixed-motive
case may be limited where the employer demonstrates that it would have made
the same decision in the absence of the unlawful motive.
