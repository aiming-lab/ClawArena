# Part II — Employment Classification

## 2.1 Employee vs. Independent Contractor

The legal characterization of a working relationship as one of employment or
independent contracting is determined by reference to the substance of the
relationship, not its form. Courts and administrative tribunals apply a
multi-factor test that examines, among other things: the degree of control
exercised by the engaging party over the manner and means of work; the
provision of tools and equipment; the integration of the worker into the
engaging party's business; the existence of a separate business undertaking on
the part of the worker; and the parties' expressed intent.

A label of "independent contractor" assigned by the engaging party is not
determinative. Misclassification exposes the engaging party to liability for
unpaid wages, overtime, social-insurance contributions, and statutory benefits.

## 2.2 Exempt and Non-Exempt Status

Under the prevailing wage and hour framework, employees are classified as
exempt or non-exempt for the purposes of overtime compensation. Exempt status
is generally available only to employees who satisfy both (i) a duties test —
typically requiring the performance of executive, administrative, or
professional duties as the primary occupation — and (ii) a salary basis test
that requires payment of a guaranteed minimum salary not subject to
deductions for variations in the quality or quantity of work performed.

Employees who do not satisfy both tests must be classified as non-exempt and
are entitled to overtime at the statutory rate for hours worked in excess of
the applicable weekly threshold.

## 2.3 Full-Time, Part-Time, and Temporary

The full-time / part-time / temporary distinction is, in general, a matter of
contractual definition rather than statutory mandate, but it carries legal
consequences in three principal areas: (a) eligibility for statutory
benefits, where the statute applies a numerical hours-worked threshold;
(b) calculation of pro rata leave entitlements; and (c) application of
performance management procedures that distinguish among employee classes,
such as the differential minimum PIP duration discussed in Part V.

## 2.4 Probationary Employees

A probationary period is a contractually defined initial term during which the
employer may apply a streamlined assessment and separation procedure. The
existence of a probationary period does not displace statutory
anti-discrimination, anti-retaliation, or wage-and-hour protections, all of
which attach from the first day of employment.
